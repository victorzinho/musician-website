**imogen** is a prototype that lets AI agents edit musical scores. Musicians talk to it about music; underneath, the agent
is writing code. That is the whole trick, and the reason it works.

[Orchestrate by prompt](/en/articles/imogen-orchestrate-by-prompt/) is the musician's side: what it's for, in their terms, with
both demos. This one is about the engineering, and where it could go.

The parts a real product would stand on are robust and carefully built; what's missing is missing only because this
is a demo. [Which are which](#what-it-takes-to-build) is at the end.

## Agents that write code

Behind the musical conversation, Claude Opus 5.5 is editing a text file, sometimes writing a small program to make one
change across many bars. To the agent, orchestrating is a programming task. That matters because programming is what
AI agents are being pushed hard to do well right now, and every step the industry takes there, imogen takes too.

The first results feel familiar to anyone who has asked an AI to write code. A dozen lines of style rules for
Debussy were enough for the [demo](/en/articles/imogen-orchestrate-by-prompt/#a-melody-orchestrated): it did what I
asked, but it wasn't what I would have written and definitely not good enough for a professional score.
Code written without guidance starts the same way: it works, it looks good, but if you spend enough time reviewing it
you notice it might start taking the codebase in a direction you don't want.

Like many of us, I write software with agents under strict and structured rules for coding (how a project is laid out,
how code is named, tested and documented), written once and loaded every time; today, with little effort from my side,
you can't tell my code from the agent's. A business with a house style can do the same: write its rules once,
and "orchestrate it like Debussy" ends up meaning exactly what it means to them.

## Why not MusicXML

The obvious thing to hand an agent is MusicXML, which every notation program reads and writes. It's the wrong thing.
It has to carry whatever any notation program needs to redraw its pages (positions, fonts, spacing, system breaks), all
mixed in with the notes. Every program reads and writes it a little differently: a study at McGill (2019) found that
converting between score formats introduced discrepancies **every** time. And it's huge. A movement of a Beethoven string
quartet takes 1.4 MB of MusicXML; the same music as plain text is about 60 KB, over twenty times smaller. The melody
in the first demo went from 32 KB to about 1 KB.

So imogen gives the agent a text that holds the music and nothing else: notes, rhythms, dynamics, articulations, slurs,
text, instruments and their transpositions, one part after another. It's as terse as it can be while still holding
everything a musician means by "the music". When the agent is done, it goes back to MusicXML; whatever imogen can't
read, it reports by part and bar.

## The music apart from the score

Under the text is a model of the music in Python: notes, bars, keys, instruments with their ranges and transpositions,
slurs and dynamics, and nothing about how a page looks. Because it holds the music in a musician's terms, operations
on it are short programs:

- write a B♭ clarinet part from concert pitch, or move a passage to another key;
- check every note against its instrument's range before a player ever sees it;
- flag parallel fifths, voice crossings, or a bassoon above a given note in quiet passages.

Checks like these matter because the agent can't hear, and models are still weak at rhythm: a 2025 study found them
answering none of its rhythm questions right, in any format. So the work splits: hard rules run as code on every edit,
the agent reviews the softer ones against stricter instructions, and a musician's ear has the last word.

None of this is a new idea. SMDL, an ISO standard from the 1990s, already separated the logical music, which it called
the *cantus*, from its engraving, its performance and its analysis; it was hardly ever implemented. Wiggins, Miranda,
Smaill and Harris (1993) compared music representations by how much they can express and how general they are.
imogen's model is that logical layer for one tradition: Western notated music.

## What it takes to build

The honest answer: the hard part works, and the product around it is still to be built.

**The foundations are solid.** The format and the model of the music are the part I'd defend in any review. Every
element of MusicXML 4.0 (and there are **a lot**) is classified, one by one, as music to keep, music to add later,
or layout to drop. A score exported and imported again comes back unchanged, and every export validates against the
MusicXML schema. And the question the whole project hinged on has its answer: AI can work on music by writing code.
As a musician, I'm happy with what it does today; as an engineer, I know what more rules will do to it, because
I've seen them do it to code.

**The product needs work.**

- **Paying for it.** Every request to the agent costs AI tokens. Someone has to pay for them, and a musician should see
  that as a subscription or a price, with accounts and limits around it.
- **No hands on the controls.** Today I start each agent session myself, by hand.
- **Agents on their own.** An agent that edits files, and writes and runs small programs to do it, needs serious
  security once nobody is watching it: sealed off from everything but the score it's working on, with limits on what
  it can run. Today I watch every session.
- **The notation it doesn't cover yet.** Lyrics, chord symbols, tremolos, harmonics, pedalling, percussion and octave
  lines, among others. Nothing in the model is in their way: the demos didn't need them, so they aren't built yet.
- **Where it lives.** Its own web app, or inside a notation program, a DAW or a publisher's platform: a product
  decision that shapes everything else.

How long that takes depends on what the product is, and deciding that comes first. What I can say is that the risky
part, whether AI can work on music at all, is behind us.

For the journey so far, I'm glad to be both a senior engineer and a musician: here it meant knowing what counts as
"musical intent" across the whole MusicXML schema, or why a band director wants a transcription, as well as how to
build the thing.
