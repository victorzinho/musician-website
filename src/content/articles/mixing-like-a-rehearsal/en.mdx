In any rehearsal, whether orchestra, choir or band, there's a moment that keeps coming back: the conductor stops, asks
for just the cellos from bar forty and asks them for a different articulation, to blend better with the percussion.
Then everyone plays again.

Mixing in a DAW is very similar: listening to the whole, finding what doesn't work, isolating it, fixing it and putting
it back together. When I started mixing I wasn't starting from zero, because I had hundreds of hours in rehearsal rooms
behind me and many more of practice. I had to learn to use the tools, but I already knew what to listen for.

This is how I mix, with a real example: a passage from my orchestral work
[*Turbulence in Circles*](/en/works/turbulence-in-circles/), before and after mixing.

## Practising at home

You arrive at a rehearsal prepared. Sometimes you have the part beforehand and sometimes you're handed it as you sit
down and you sight-read, but the same is always expected: that you play what's written, with its dynamics and its
articulations, so the rehearsal can go to the whole and not to each part.

In a mockup, that's editing. The MIDI I export from the Sibelius score has the notes, but it doesn't sound like anyone
playing. I edit each track, one by one, until it does: dynamics and expression, changes from pizzicato to arco,
tremolos. For the oboe I had to use two different virtual instruments, because I couldn't find one that did both normal
phrasing and the multiphonics the score asks for well.

When all the tracks sound right on their own, I render them to audio and normalise them so they're all at a reasonable,
similar level. There's no mix to respect yet, so it's the most neutral starting point.

## Seating the orchestra

Before listening to anything, I place each instrument on the stage: left or right, front or back. It's the same thing a
conductor decides when seating an orchestra, and it's also decided before the rehearsal.

Second violins, for example, can go on the right, opposite the firsts, so they stand apart when they answer each other;
or beside them, so they blend. In *Turbulence in Circles* I put them together. The piece isn't about motifs passing from
one voice to another but about textures blending, like the trumpet with the oboe or the violin harmonics with the
flutes, and the first and second violins often play a single texture.

## The first run-through, in pencil

With everything in place, I take the score and a pencil and listen to the whole mockup without touching the computer.
Every problem goes on the score, at its bar and on its instrument.

I do it this way because it forces me to work in musical terms, which I handle better. On the one hand, I listen to the
whole piece, because many things depend on what's happening around them or in other sections. On the other, what gets
written down is still music: “the trumpet isn't with the oboe in bar 37” says what's wrong and what to achieve, which is
what I need to know before deciding how to fix it.

I usually listen three times. In the first I find almost everything, but while I'm writing I miss things; in the second
I write down what I heard and didn't get to note; in the third I check that everything I hear already has its
annotation. If the music is very dense I need a few more, and if it's clear two are enough.

## What the annotations say

Many came up in *Turbulence in Circles*. Some were general: the strings were too loud throughout the passage. Others
were balances between instruments, the kind that come up in a rehearsal:

- In bars 37 to 42, the trumpet has to blend with the oboe, and it was falling below it.
- In bar 47, the clarinets are accompaniment, and they were too loud.
- In bar 52, the bassoon is marked *pp*, but it's a motif and has to be heard, even if it's quiet.

Others wouldn't come up in a rehearsal: the timpani had a resonance that caused problems with other instruments. That's
a problem of the sound in the mix, not of the music, and it can only be fixed with technique, in this case EQ.

With the list done, I go back to the computer and work through the annotations one by one. Then I repeat the process as
many times as it takes until I'm satisfied.

## When the music is someone else's

In a mockup of mine, the score and the decisions are mine. When I mix someone else's music, their score is in charge,
just as it is for a conductor.

I'm currently producing the recording of the musical *Yo mataré miedos por ti* from the composer's scores. We've worked
together before and we trust each other, so I sometimes try something that isn't in the score, like bringing the
woodwinds up a little in a passage; I make a note of it, mention it at our next session and, if they don't like it, we
change it. With someone I don't have that trust with yet, I try to ask before starting: “Do you want me to bring ideas,
or to mix what you give me, as it is?”

## Knowing what you're there for

A rehearsal is planned around the time available. An hour might only be enough to get one section right or to give
general directions; with a whole morning you can go through the piece from top to bottom and get into detail in
different places. A good conductor decides that before walking in.

Mixing is the same. Ears tire the way musicians tire, and after a while I hear detail less well. An hour before leaving
the house isn't the same as a whole afternoon with a coffee beside me. Because the annotations are all on the score, I
can choose which ones fit in today's session.

And a mix has a date, like a concert: it has to be ready for that day, even if there's always something left to improve.

## The what and the how

For the trumpet and the oboe in bars 37 to 42 I knew what timbre I was after, because I'd heard it before: the muted
trumpet and the oboe in *Winter Sky*, by Kaija Saariaho. That came from being a musician. Getting it in the mix is
something else: you start with the levels, but sometimes you need EQ, or to move one of the two slightly on the stage.
That's mixing technique, and I had to learn it like anyone else.

From rehearsals I brought the what: hearing the whole, knowing where the problem is. The how I had to learn, the way you
learn the technical side of orchestration, with theory and practice. But you learn it much faster when you already know
what you're after and can recognise when you have it.
