**imogen** brings the power of AI to music notation: you talk to an
agent about a score, in bars, instruments and dynamics, and you see and hear it change. You shape it together, round
after round, for as long as you want; when it's where you want it, it goes into your own notation software.

Two sessions show it: one orchestrates a melody of mine, the other turns a DAW mockup into a score for a recording
session.

## A melody, orchestrated

<YouTube id="fVJupgX-QQI" title="imogen: a melody orchestrated by prompt">A melody of mine, orchestrated by prompt</YouTube>

I wrote a short melody, exported it as MusicXML (the file every notation program can save) and asked for it like
Debussy. All the agent knew about Debussy was its own memory and this:

<Prompt>

- Instruments: two flutes, two oboes, one English horn, two clarinets, two bassoons, four horns, a harp, glockenspiel (optional) and strings.
- The melody:
  - For woodwinds use one staff (with two voices and/or "a 2" indications) or two staves if the voices are too different. French horns always in two staves.
  - Appears often in woodwinds: flute in the low register, cor anglais, clarinet.
  - Might pass it between instruments but not double it.
  - Might adjust it in terms of rhythm and articulation (not pitch) to suit the style better.
- Strings:
  - For soft passages, often muted (sordino).
  - Might use divisi.
- Harmony:
  - Lean *a lot* into chords built by stacked fourths.
  - Avoid functional harmony: chords happen because of their colour and not
    their function (no IV-V-I).
  - Dominant chords, if they ever appear, don't resolve.
  - The first and last chords don't necessarily have to be the tonic.
- A lot of attention to articulation detail, written as expression text in the score.

</Prompt>

Then like Mozart, with these rules:

<Prompt>

- Instruments: two flutes, two oboes, two bassoons, two horns, two trumpets, timpani and strings.
  - For winds use one staff (with two voices and/or "a 2" indications).
  - Strings are the centrepiece of the orchestration.
  - Woodwinds often add colour to the strings.
  - Horns usually play a blending role between strings and woodwinds.
  - Trumpets are often reserved for specific energetic passages.
- The melody:
  - Always in violins. Might double it with woodwind instruments.
- Harmony:
  - Functional harmony only.
  - Dominant chords must happen and resolve to the tonic.
  - First and last chords are the tonic.
  - Notes outside the key are only secondary dominants, Neapolitan sixths or ornaments.

</Prompt>

Then I asked for smaller changes, by bar and by instrument, until I was happy with the result.

## A DAW mockup, ready for the session

Mockups are played into a DAW by hand, against a click. Export one as MIDI and you get a score nobody can read: tracks
called "Track 3", no dynamics, triplets turned into something else, notes a hair off the beat. Turning that into clean
parts for a recording session is hours of copying, usually on a deadline.

## Talk it through, then take it home

Working with imogen has two phases. First, you and the agent: you see the score and hear it, but you don't edit it directly.
You say what you want, the agent changes it, and you go round as many times as you like. Then you download it as
MusicXML and open it in Dorico, Sibelius or MuseScore, with real instruments, parts and transpositions: an ordinary
score again, yours to edit, print and hand out.

## How it works

The agent in the demos is [Claude Opus 5.5](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/models/opus-5-5/overview). imogen does the rest:

1. It reads your MusicXML or MIDI file and keeps only the music: notes, rhythms, dynamics, articulations, slurs, text,
   instruments. Page layout, fonts and positions stay out.
2. It writes that music as a short text, as terse as it can be while still holding everything a musician would call
   *the music*. That text is what the agent reads and changes.
3. Every time the agent changes it, imogen turns it back into a score, shows it and plays it.

The agent never sees a MusicXML file. Why that matters, and what else is under the hood, is in [Writing music as
code](/en/articles/imogen-music-as-code/).

imogen is a prototype built for these videos, not a product (yet). Some steps are manual: I start each agent session myself,
and the browser hands my messages over to it. Unfortunately there is nothing to try online, and there won't be until it's
built properly ([what that takes](/en/articles/imogen-music-as-code/#what-it-takes-to-build)).

### What the agent was told

Apart from the rules above, the agent was only pointed to what it had to read and told how to talk:

<Prompt>

Speak to the musician only in English, using musical terminology only. Never mention files, tools, code, rules or the environment.

Be collaborative:
- When an instruction has a meaningful musical ambiguity, ask one concise musical question or offer one or two concrete musical alternatives.
- Ask at most two consecutive clarification rounds.
- If ambiguity remains after the second round, make a conservative musical choice, edit the score, and state the choice plus optional brief follow-up questions.
- This is iterative: write a plausible realization matching the composer’s intent, then refine it through later requests; perfection on the first pass is not required.

</Prompt>

## Who it's for

- **Orchestration.** A melody or a sketch orchestrated for the ensemble you write for, in the style you describe. Or a
  whole orchestral work transcribed for concert band, a staple in Valencia, where nearly every town has one.
- **Producers and copyists.** From the DAW mockup to parts for the session, as in the second video.
- **The players you have.** A school orchestra without an oboe, a singer who needs it a third lower, which changes
  things for other instruments, second clarinets who can't go above the staff yet: the same piece, rewritten for the
  people in the room.
- **Reductions.** A piano reduction of an orchestral score for choir or musical rehearsals.
- **Students who want to write.** For young musicians itching to write before they've studied composition: a playful
  way to try an idea, hear it played by an orchestra and change it on the spot. It doesn't replace writing by hand or
  learning the craft; it might be what makes them want to.
- **Composition and harmony classes.** The mechanical rules (instrument ranges, parallel fifths, voices out of their
  register) can be checked before an exercise reaches the teacher. Student and teacher spend less of their time
  together on what simply can't be, and more on the music.
- **Musicology.** Scores as data: search a whole corpus for a progression, a voicing or an instrument's register, or
  compare two editions of a work as music alone, [without reading a page](/en/articles/imogen-music-as-code/#the-music-apart-from-the-score).
- **Ensembles for weddings and events.** A string quartet needs an arrangement of a melody by Saturday. They can tell a
  good arrangement from a bad one, but writing one isn't their job and there's no time.

## If you're a composer

Then it will never write what you would have written, and it isn't meant to. Some composers
write straight into Dorico, some never touch a computer, and both are fine. This is one more option for whoever finds
it useful: a first pass to react to, or an extra pair of hands for what you'd rather not type.
