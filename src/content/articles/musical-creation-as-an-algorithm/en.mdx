This is a summary of *La creación musical como algoritmo*, my thesis for the Degree in Composition at the Conservatorio
Superior de Música Joaquín Rodrigo (Valencia, 2025), supervised by Iluminada Pérez Troya. The full text, in Spanish, is
linked above as a PDF.

---

Can you compose with algorithms without losing control of the music? The question came to me from two sides. I have
written software for a living for more than fifteen years, a job that trains you to spot costly processes and judge
whether automating them pays off. At the conservatory I met works with no metre and no tonality, and composing without
those known structures felt, to some extent, like a leap into the void. I wanted a process to lean on, defined enough to
give the music coherence.

Generating music with software is no longer in question; keeping control of it is. The thesis starts from a hypothesis:
if composing is described as transforming something we perceive into musical parameters, the composer can keep control
of what is generated, as long as the process leaves room for the composer's own contribution.

The article loosely follows the thesis. First, [a few definitions](#composing-as-a-transformation), a look at
[history](#transformations-through-history) and some thoughts on [artificial intelligence](#artificial-intelligence).
Then the experiments, on three of my own works: [two on generating material](#the-problem-of-time), and one on [the
limits of humans and machines](#the-limits-of-the-human). Last, [what I concluded](#conclusions).

## Composing as a transformation

The idea the thesis puts to the test is that composing is, to a large extent, translating: something we perceive in the
world (the size of an animal, the amount of wheat in a field) becomes a musical parameter (an instrument, a pitch). If
that translation can be described precisely, it might be automated without losing control. Describing it takes only a
few words from statistics. Both what is perceived and what is musical take values of four kinds:

- **Nominal**: categories with no order (oboe, violin).
- **Ordinal**: categories with an order (*piano* is softer than *forte*).
- **Discrete**: numbers in steps (pitch in semitones).
- **Continuous**: any number (pitch in hertz).

Each type turns into another through a different operation. Between categories and numbers in steps, an **assignment**
picks a value for each value, one by one: large animals (ordinal) to percussion, small ones to strings (nominal). It is
arbitrary, which is precisely what lets it jump from anything perceived to anything musical. Continuous values need two
more. An **interpolation** goes from discrete to continuous: it fills in the values missing between known ones, like
estimating the wheat between two measured points of a field. A **classification** goes the other way, from continuous to
categories or steps: it cuts a continuous range into bands, the way semitones cut the continuum of frequency.

Chained together, these operations make an **algorithm** in the broad sense the thesis uses: not necessarily code, but a
recipe, a set of rules that turns what is perceived into music step by step. Some of the experiments below ran as
software; others were followed by hand, on paper.

## Transformations through history

In Ancient Greece, music was thought to carry *ethos*, the power to shape character: Plato ties courage to the Dorian
mode and lament to the Lydian. It is an assignment between categories: traits of character to modes, rhythms or
instruments. Its arbitrariness was at the heart of much of the Greek discussion of music, which sought to ground those
associations so that they weren't mere whim. In Gregorian chant, mode and rhythm serve the sacred text, and the
transformation aims to be minimal so that the message arrives intact.

From the Renaissance on, what is translated shifts from the divine message to human emotion. Monteverdi cites Plato and
Boethius to link passions to qualities of the voice; Mattheson writes that joy, an expansion of the soul, is best
expressed by large intervals; later, keys gather associations, such as F major with the pastoral or D minor with death.
Romantic programme music multiplies the extramusical content: Berlioz's *idée fixe*, Wagner's leitmotifs, Prokofiev's
instruments for animals, Berg's C major chord for money in *Wozzeck*. More things to translate, but still categories,
and still assigned.

The 20th century brings two changes. Integral serialism derives every parameter from a numeric series. And computers
open two lines: sound itself as material (Stockhausen, spectralism, Vivier's *jeux de couleurs*) and algorithms taking
the centre stage, with Xenakis's stochastic processes, for example.

## Artificial intelligence

A system that learns from its environment and produces music fits the same description: it transforms what it perceives
into music through what it has learned. What matters is how much room it leaves the composer.

As of today (the thesis is from 2025), a strong line of **generative AI**, aims at finished works with little or minimal
human input, trained mostly without supervision on recorded audio, with the idea of a musical Turing test as the goal.
Beyond its ethical and energy problems, that use reduces the composer's contribution to almost nothing, which places it
outside the thesis.

AI in **computer-assisted composition** does leave that room for creation: IRCAM's Orchidea, given a target sound, searches
for the orchestral combination that sounds most alike (Cella 2022); DYCI2 improvises with musicians from a corpus of
classified sounds (Nika et al. 2017). The composer chooses the target or the corpus, and uses the result like any other
material.

Generative AI also reopens an old question: is music a product or an activity? The original Turing test was a
conversation, not the comparison of finished texts (Ariza 2009). As Christopher Small put it: "Music is not a thing at
all but an activity, something that people do" (Small 1998).

## The problem of time

If music is sound organised in time, its hardest problem, wherever a discourse is expected, is managing material across
a work: what comes next, and when it changes. Forms such as the sonata answer in part; integral serialism answers with
rules, at times at the cost of an erratic discourse; generative AI, in 2025, still struggled to stay coherent over time.

Two experiments addressed one question each. Each was judged on what it costs to set up (writing code, for instance),
what it costs to turn its output into a finished work, how audible the process is, and how satisfying overall.

### What comes next: -90.938, 42.3 – 2017

This piece for three marimbas translates a process that is already coherent in the real world: a season in a wheat
field. Farm machinery recorded, at each point of its path, the seeds sown, the product sprayed and the crop harvested,
and satellite images gave the vegetation. Each point became a sixteenth note: the three measurements, continuous values,
were classified into pitch classes, and the vegetation set the key, turning notes outside it into rests.  From the lines
generated, I then wrote, freely, one marimba line per machine.

<Figure src={fieldGenerated} alt="Nine staves of generated sixteenth notes, labelled by variable and classification algorithm">
  The first three bars as generated: seeding, crop protection and yield, each classified with three algorithms.
</Figure>

<Figure src={fieldMarimbas} alt="Three marimba staves, the same three bars as written from the generated lines">
  The same three bars, written for the three marimbas.
</Figure>

The machinery's measurements can't be heard without an explanation: an abrupt change in one passage could be anything in
the field. Implementing the processing from scratch is expensive; here it wasn't, because I was developing that same
algorithm at work, to display vegetation on maps, and the part this experiment needs is now public. Refinement stayed
costly: the process gives harmony and little else, and dynamics, articulation, dialogue and form were all still to be
decided. The success of the piece depends almost entirely on the composer; its value was personal, a way out of
established structures and of the *horror vacui* of composing without known patterns.

It also exposed a gap: the harmony ignores the time the machine spent at each point, so cadences, transitions and
tension are left to chance, and the composer ends up shaping a sequence that was never generated with a form in mind.

### When it changes: Kissed by God

[*Kissed by God*](/en/works/kissed-by-god/) takes its large-scale form from a surf session, an activity with a small set
of actions and very characteristic proportions: watching the sea, paddling out, misjudging a wave, paddling back, being
held under by another, paddling back again, and finally riding one. From them come four themes: watching the sea;
paddling, out (rising) or to catch a wave (falling); the wipeout; and triumph. Sections, their character and the
transitions between them follow the session, and the plan was drawn before any note was written:

<Figure src={surfPlan} alt="Hand-drawn plan: tempo, keys and dynamics over a seven-minute timeline, with sections a, b, wipeout and triumph">
  The composition plan: tempo, keys, playing techniques and dynamics over seven minutes, under the session's phases
  and the resulting form.
</Figure>

The main transformations:

- **Time (continuous) → time (continuous).** At first a plain scaling of the session's proportions; it had to adapt.
- **Distance from the shore (continuous) → pitch class (discrete).** The shore set as A♭ and the best take-off points as
  E♭ and F, interpolated between them and rounded to semitones.
- **Lack of air, chaos (nominal) → randomness, sudden changes of dynamics and register (nominal).** An arbitrary
  assignment, chosen so that the frenzy of the wipeout feels as close as possible to the real thing.
- **Heart rate (continuous) → pulse (continuous).** Also meant as a direct translation, and also adapted.

Again, the session can't be recovered from the music. Some sensation may come through, softened, as in the wipeout, but
the video carries most of the translation. With nothing to compute, the process costs nothing beyond composing; a
programmatic plan helps order ideas and get past a block, but these transformations add nothing audible and save no
work.

<YouTube id="O06FQ8ONp9s" title="Kissed by God">*Kissed by God* (2024), for piano, video and electronics.</YouTube>

### The problem of proportions

In the water, waiting is disproportionately long next to paddling or riding, so the plan was corrected from the start,
and again while writing, by musical judgement alone.

Kaija Saariaho faced a similar problem in *Orion*. In its second movement, *Winter Sky*, the length of each section
follows a numeric plan, beats against tempo; yet where a slower tempo would have stretched one section beyond its place
in the plan, she cut six beats from it. Even composers of her stature adjust their numeric plans when the music asks for
it; my own adjustments, at a different scale, answer the same question.

Deciding *what* comes next can be formalised and kept under control, though the cost may exceed the work it saves.
Deciding *when* the material changes involves an arbitrariness, often shared and rooted in our artistic context, that
resists formalisation. Algorithms can help, but guarantee nothing: "hope of an extraordinary aesthetic success based on
extraordinary technology is a cruel deceit" (Xenakis 1985).

## The limits of the human

[*Ese Mi parece un Mi♭*](/en/works/ese-mi-parece-un-mi-bemol/), for fretless electric guitar and electronics, treats
pitch and time as continuous, and asks where the limits lie: what performers and electronics can play, what listeners
can hear, and how to write it down.

### Pitch as a continuous parameter

Any pitch should be possible, yet pitches need relations to build a discourse rather than an erratic sequence. The
starting conditions: notes repeat at the octave; fifths are pure (702 cents, against 700 in equal temperament); thirds
are ambiguous, halfway between major and minor (340 cents); C is the reference, and whatever applies to C applies to G.

Two more chords were found by ear on a synthesiser, to give the harmony direction: a tenser one, with a low tritone and
a sixth as ambiguous as the thirds, and a "dominant" of two stacked low tritones that resolves back to C.

<Figure src={tuningChords} alt="Eight chords on a staff in two groups, labelled Do and Sol, with microtonal accidentals">
  The basic chords of the tuning, on C (*Do*) and on G (*Sol*); the G chord belongs to both.
</Figure>

The remaining gaps were filled in so that no step exceeds a semitone: 19 unequal notes per octave.

Then comes precision. Listeners tell apart pitch differences of about 3 to 6 cents (Loeffler 2006; Olson 1967), and
producing a pitch on an instrument is necessarily harder than hearing it. An A at 906 cents against one at 900 is at the
edge of perception, and beyond what a composer can reasonably ask of a performer. So the electronics play every pitch
exactly, while the score keeps notation as conventional as possible, with the real cents as a reference:

<Figure src={tuningScore} alt="The score's tuning instructions: 19 notes on a staff with their cents, and notes on the accidentals">
  The tuning, as explained at the start of the score.
</Figure>

### Time as a continuous parameter

Time has its own established proportions: halves and thirds of a beat mean something to every trained listener. The
piece avoids them in two ways.

First, treating time as a continuous line. The electronics place events at random clustering organically; the guitar
part has no barlines, only noteheads at distances proportional to time. With no recognisable
relation between events, the section has no pulse, however dense it gets.

<Figure src={atemporal} alt="Guitar and electronics staves without barlines, with time marked in seconds">
  The section without pulse: events placed in seconds, not beats.
</Figure>

Second, using proportions outside our tradition. Plotting eighths, sixths and fifths of a beat shows how little room is
left between them, and how close those gaps are to the limit of what we can perceive or play:

<Figure src={subdivisions} alt="A line from 0 to 1 with points marking eighths, sixths and fifths of a beat">
  Eighths (red dots), sixths (blue circles) and fifths (green crosses) of a beat.
</Figure>

Subdividing the beat ever further is already well explored, down to what Adam Neely and Shawn Crowder call
*hypertuplets* (Neely 2021, 2024; Crowder 2021). The piece looks elsewhere: a subdivision that doesn't add up to the
beat, so that two quavers last more or less than a crotchet. The ratio, chosen arbitrarily, is 1 quaver = 0.6 crotchets.

That raises two problems. The first is writing it down. We discriminate musical events as short as about 100
milliseconds (London 2002), and we performers can reach that precision from a traditional notation that has been in use
for centuries. Inventing a notation would throw that away, and could even be counterproductive; writing the relation
with a pulse and known figures, however complex, is more productive. The beat is written as five semiquavers and the
subdivision as three (3 / 5 = 0.6):

<Figure src={complexSubdivisions} alt="Guitar and electronics staves in a 5+12+5+6 over 16 time signature">
  The 0.6 subdivision, written as three semiquavers against a beat of five.
</Figure>

The second is the performer's deviations, deliberate (a *ritardando*, a fermata) or not. Electronics can place events to
the millisecond, but can't follow a performer as another musician would. In the piece, the electronics are a
pre-recorded track written to double as the guitarist's click, with cues to measure the complex relations.

<YouTube id="q6tZGH8PJuI" title="Ese Mi parece un Mi♭">*Ese Mi parece un Mi♭* (2022), for fretless electric guitar and electronics: studio recording.</YouTube>

Machines have more resolution than our perception, but can't adapt to a live performance; performers are less precise,
though a professional comes very close to the limits of perception, and they react at once. And traditional notation,
with as few additions as possible, proved the most effective.

## Conclusions

- **History prefers categories.** Numbers were always there, but they and complex processing only take centre stage in
  the 20th century, with computers and a growing attention to sound itself as material.
- **Control depends on the tool.** Generative AI aimed at finished works takes most of it away; designing the process
  yourself keeps it all.
- **Technology guarantees nothing.** Generated material takes real time to become a finished work, so the work saved is
  small. Its value is elsewhere: like sketching on paper or at the piano, it leads to material one wouldn't reach
  otherwise.
- **The algorithm shouldn't be heard.** An algorithm is deterministic: when the listener can always tell what comes
  next, the music loses its capacity to surprise.

This thesis is also where my two trades began to meet. Writing software taught me to formalise processes; composing
taught me which decisions shouldn't be. Any tool for musicians draws that line somewhere, between what the machine
decides and what stays with the person using it, and having drawn it from the composer's side, in my own pieces, is what
I bring to the tools I build. The code behind these experiments is [public](
https://github.com/victorzinho/brain-fartools), along with [browser
tools](https://victorzinho.github.io/brain-fartools/) I use while composing.
