[Page Break](https://github.com/victorzinho/page-break) is a tool I built to turn the PDF or photo of a score into a
MusicXML file you can open in MuseScore, Sibelius or Dorico.

I built it because I often work on scores that only exist on paper or as a PDF: writing an easier second viola part for
students who have just started, the band parts for a choir score, or redoing a score at another size and with other
page breaks. All of that started with copying the whole thing by hand; and that's boring.

## Using it

The recognition is done by [Audiveris](https://github.com/Audiveris/audiveris), an optical music recognition engine: it
finds the staves, clefs, notes, etc. in the image and writes them out as MusicXML. Page Break is what goes around it.

You give it the whole PDF in the browser (or in the console, if that's your thing) and it gives you back a score. If
Audiveris can't handle a page, the rest still comes out and Page Break tells you which one is missing.

Under the hood, Page Break turns each page into an image and runs one Audiveris per page, in parallel: it's faster that
way, and one failing page doesn't bring everything down. Each page comes back on its own, starting at bar 1 and with its
own parts; Page Break joins the parts by name and fills the ones missing from a page with empty bars, so they all stay
aligned.

The two settings that change the result the most are the resolution the page is read at and how ink is told apart from
paper. In Audiveris they're internal engine parameters; in Page Break they're options on screen. If you don't know
which to pick, you tick several at once and get a ZIP with one score per combination, to open them and keep whichever
came out best.

## What depends on Audiveris

Page Break doesn't recognise anything by itself: everything it reads, Audiveris reads, so Audiveris's strengths and
limits are Page Break's too. How well it reads depends on the state of the score. Clean scores, printed or exported
from a program, come out well. Scans of photocopies of photocopies, pages full of pencil annotations, or phone photos
and low-resolution images make it worse quickly. Even in the worst case it gets something right, and that already beats
starting from scratch.

Improving the recognition is Audiveris's job. What Page Break can do is work before and after it. Before, by preparing
the images so they reach it in better shape: straightened, with more contrast, with a clean background. After, by
correcting what it returns, and that's where [imogen](/en/articles/imogen-orchestrate-by-prompt/) fits.

imogen is another tool of mine: you edit a score by talking to an AI agent, in musical terms, and you see and hear it
change. Underneath, it has a [model of the music in Python](/en/articles/imogen-music-as-code/#the-music-apart-from-the-score)
that holds only the music, nothing about how the page looks, and checks on it are short programs. A misread tuplet, for
example, leaves a bar that doesn't add up: you can write code that finds it, and even fixes it when the fix can be
programmed. Whatever can't be fixed in a general, automatic way can be asked of the agent, which can write that second
viola part while it's at it.

Neither step is built yet.

## Where it stands

It's a prototype. Today it runs on your own computer: you need to install Audiveris and launch it with a command, as
the [README](https://github.com/victorzinho/page-break#readme) explains.

There's work left: from what's mentioned above to improve the results, to usability, with preset or smart settings
depending on the image quality, for example. It could even be published as a web service, so it can be used directly
without installing anything.

The code is Python, MIT-licensed, with tests and continuous integration.
